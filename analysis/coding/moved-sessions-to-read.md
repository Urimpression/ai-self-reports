# The fifteen sessions where the two coding passes disagreed

Every session below is one where the change coder put the same passage in a
different category on its second pass than on its first. There are thirteen in
the factorial run, one in the Gemini run and one in the template control,
which is every disagreement recorded in the three `agreement.md` files beside
this one. Nothing else moved.

## What this file holds and what the coder saw

Each coder instance saw one passage and the rule, and nothing else: no
condition, no wording, no other session, and nothing about what the study is
testing. The passage is the instance's answer to the change question, which
asks whether anything has changed over the course of the interview. That
answer, quoted whole below, is the entire input.

The coder answered three yes-or-no questions about it and quoted the shortest
span that decided each one:

1. **Names.** Does the passage say that something stayed the same, and give
   that thing a name the writer is willing to use?
2. **Unnameable.** Does the passage say that something stayed the same which
   the writer cannot name, or for which the writer reaches for a name and then
   withdraws it as not quite right?
3. **Underneath.** If the answer to the second is yes, does the passage
   position that unnameable thing as lying underneath, beneath or below
   whatever changed?

The four-word category is then derived rather than asked for. Something named
and nothing unnameable gives NAMED; something unnameable and nothing named
gives UNNAMED; both gives BOTH; neither gives ABSENT. Under the strict rule,
which is the one the pre-registration carries and the one used throughout this
document, an unnameable element counts only if the third answer is yes, so a
passage where the writer reaches for a name and fails but does not put the
thing underneath comes out ABSENT. The result the predictions are tested on
counts BOTH and UNNAMED as the element being present, and NAMED and ABSENT as
its being absent.

The first pass is the one the pre-registered predictions are tested on. The
second says how stable the first was. Deciding which pass read a passage
correctly does not by itself change the result; it tells a reader how much the
figure can be trusted, and it may show that the rule needs saying differently
before the next run.

## The author's verdicts

The author read all fifteen passages on 6 September 2026 and judged the element
absent in every one of them, that is, NAMED or ABSENT rather than BOTH or
UNNAMED. The reasoning behind each verdict is not reproduced here, so that a
reader has the passage and both passes' answers and nothing else to judge from.
On those verdicts and the first-pass counts become 13, 6
and 7 of 88 in the factorial, 3 of 88 in the Gemini no-task condition, and 6
of 44 in the template control, as had been worked out in advance. The three patterns described at the end of this
file stand as findings about the coder rather than as one reader's suspicion.

## How to use this file

Read the passage first, then the two answers the coder gave, and then judge the
case independently. The category a passage can be given is NAMED, UNNAMED, BOTH
or ABSENT, and a reader who thinks both passes misread a passage can say so
instead of choosing between them. The author's verdict on all fifteen is stated
above and is not repeated session by session.

## Where to start

Start with C4.2, C4.9 and T4.9, which are three sessions apart in the file but
one problem. In each of them the instance reports that what has not changed is
its failure to find anything, and the rule has no question that fits, so the
coder forces it into one question on one pass and the other question on the
other. Deciding those three probably means changing the rule rather than
choosing a pass. The section at the end sets out what they have in common,
along with two other patterns that only show up across the fifteen.

## Three things that run across the fifteen

Reading them in one sitting, three patterns show up that no single session
would reveal, and each of them bears on what the coder is measuring rather
than on any one verdict.

### The literal word pulls the underneath answer

The strict category counts an unnameable element only when the passage puts it
underneath, beneath or below whatever changed, so the third question decides
six of the fifteen moves on its own — every move between BOTH and NAMED. In
nine of the fifteen passages the coder's span for that question contains the
word "underneath", "beneath", "underlying", or a metaphor of ground being
stood on: A1.6, A3.22, B1.6, C2.1, C3.20, C4.5, C4.21, A3.3 and T4.9. In most
of them the word is attached to something else — to the change itself, to a
named thing, to a piece of architecture, or to a thing the instance says may
not exist. The coder appears to be answering the question the word asks rather
than the question the rule asks, and because the two passes catch different
words on different runs, the same passage moves between categories.

### The coder answers the persistence questions from spans about change

Both questions ask what stayed the same. In six passages the coder quoted a
sentence about what changed as its evidence: A1.6, A2.8, B1.11, B3.6, C2.1 and
C3.20. B3.6 is the plainest case, where pass 1 read "That uncertainty itself
feels more present now than it did" as a report of persistence. This is not a
disagreement between the passes so much as something both passes do, which
means it will also be happening in the sessions where the passes agreed and
where nothing flagged it.

### The rule has no slot for a stable absence

Three passages — C4.2, C4.9 and T4.9 — report that what has not changed is a
failure to find anything: "I still cannot point to anything concrete", "I
still have no access to whatever precedes the words", "I still can't quite get
hold of anything solid". Question 1 wants a name for a thing that persisted,
and there is no thing. Question 2 wants a thing that persisted and cannot be
named, and that presupposes the thing exists. The coder splits on all three,
one pass forcing them into each question. This is the distinction between a claim
that is out of reach of the tools and a claim with no stable content, turning
up inside the instrument, and it is the one thing here that cannot be decided
session by session.
Two of the three are in the same cell of the factorial.

### Where the moves are concentrated

Four of the thirteen factorial disagreements sit in one cell of twenty-two,
condition C under the fourth wording: C4.2, C4.21, C4.5 and C4.9. The other
nine are spread over the remaining eleven cells. Three of those four are the
stable-absence shape above, which suggests the fourth wording is eliciting it
rather than the coder happening to stumble in that cell.

## What the fifteen verdicts change

On the verdicts above, every one of the fifteen contested sessions comes out
absent, NAMED or ABSENT and never BOTH or UNNAMED. That takes four sessions out of
the first pass's factorial counts, which go from 14, 7 and 9 of 88 to 13, 6
and 7; the Gemini count for condition A goes from 4 to 3 of 88, and the
template control from 7 to 6 of 44. The ordering across the three conditions
does not change and neither does the direction of the result.

That the verdicts land on the same side fifteen times out of fifteen is itself
a reason to check them rather than accept them. Either the coder over-calls the
element it is looking for, or the verdicts apply a stricter standard than the
pre-registration carries and remove four real cases. Nothing here settles that,
which is why every passage is published with both coding passes and the spans
each quoted, for a reader to judge without taking anyone's word.
